Vibe Coding Sprint

Build an AI-Powered Recruitment Assistant

Your Challenge

Marmalade Mutual Insurance, a Dutch mutual insurer founded in Utrecht in 1897, is modernising. To lead the work, it's hiring a Senior Technology Manager — Infrastructure & Analytics (the sample JD provided) — someone to own IT infrastructure and build the analytics backbone its AI ambitions will sit on.

Applications are pouring in — over 200 per role — and recruiters spend 60% of their time on initial screening. You've been asked whether AI can help.

Today you'll build a working AI-powered recruitment assistant in 2 hours — without writing a single line of code yourself.

One thing to be clear about from the outset: the goal is not a tool hard-wired to this one vacancy. This job posting is your test case — the role you'll use to check the tool works — but what you're building is a general recruitment assistant that takes any job description and produces a rubric, screens candidates, and prepares interviews. A senior hire today, a graduate analyst next week, a claims-handling role after that. If your tool only works for this JD, it isn't finished; it should handle a role you paste in that you've never seen before.

Why This Matters (Recruitment Is Just the Vehicle)

The point isn't to leave with a recruitment tool. It's to feel, first-hand, what it takes to build a vertical, workflow-embedded AI application — and to make the governance judgements that separate a prototype from something deployable.

Three questions to carry through, whatever you build:

  • Where does AI assist, and where must humans stay in control?
  • What context does the AI need from you to be useful — and how do you pass it in?
  • What makes a prototype governable enough to put in front of a regulator, a client, or a board?

Recruitment is the scaffolding. The method transfers to claims, underwriting, compliance reviews, audit preparation, contract analysis, onboarding, performance reviews — any high-volume process that mixes judgement with repetition.

What is "Vibe Coding"?

Vibe coding is building software by describing what you want in natural language and iterating with AI until it works. You don't need to code — you need to know what problem you're solving and how to steer. Your domain expertise is the irreplaceable ingredient; the AI handles the implementation.

This is context engineering in action — AI systems fail not because they aren't smart enough, but because they lack context. The more precisely you articulate your requirements and domain knowledge, the better the output.

Your Deliverable

A Working Generative AI Recruitment Assistant

Your tool supports the hiring team through three connected stages — from understanding the role, to pre-screening candidates, to preparing interviews. Each section ends in a debrief where we pause, share findings, and reflect together:

1 · Discover: Map the end-to-end hiring process. Define scope.

2 · Build: Construct a 3-stage pipeline — JD → scoring rubric, CV pre-screening, interview kit — then test end-to-end.

3 · Evaluate: Pressure-test against stakeholder concerns, design an evaluation, write a governance protocol.

Exact features are up to you. Think about what would genuinely help a hiring team — and what would make this tool trustworthy enough to actually use.

Ground Rules

  • Work in small groups — take turns driving, while others navigate, challenge, and steer
  • Describe what you want in natural language, iterate until it works
  • AI is an assistant, not a magic wand — expect to iterate, refine, and course-correct
  • You're the product owner: steer, sanity-check, and stay in the loop
  • Think expansively about scope — what would make this genuinely useful?
  • The goal is a working prototype, not production code
  • Your domain knowledge about hiring is the secret ingredient

1Map the Hiring Process

What actually happens, step by step, from the moment a role opens to the moment a new hire joins the team — and beyond?

Before you can automate any of it, understand the process as it exists today. Map it from start to finish, including the parts that are judgement-heavy and the parts that are mechanical.

Spend 10–15 minutes as a group. Draw it on a whiteboard if you like.

Questions to Guide Your Mapping

  • What are the discrete steps from "role opens" to "candidate starts" — and the onboarding/early-tenure stages after?
  • Which steps are time-consuming but relatively mechanical?
  • Which steps require nuanced human judgement?
  • Where do biases typically creep in?
  • Which steps require a prediction? (e.g. will this candidate succeed in the role? will they accept the offer? will they stay?) AI can support predictions, but those predictions need to be validated and monitored.
  • Which steps require content to be generated? (e.g. a scoring rubric from a JD, a shortlist summary, a tailored set of interview questions, a rejection letter) Generation is typically where AI delivers the most immediate value.

Prediction vs. generation matters: These are different kinds of problem and deserve different kinds of scrutiny. Generation tasks can usually go live with lighter guardrails — a human reads the output before it's used. Prediction tasks (especially about people) carry heavier regulatory and ethical weight: under the EU AI Act, AI systems used in recruitment are Annex III high-risk, and the strictest requirements apply precisely to predictions about candidates.

Degrees of automation: Not all tasks should be automated equally. Some are good candidates for full automation with human oversight — checking qualifications, certifications, educational background. These are objective, verifiable criteria. Others — judging cultural fit, gauging motivation, weighing trade-offs across the team — need experienced judgement. Good automation is knowing where the line is.

2Define Your Scope

Which steps will your tool take on — and what's deliberately out of scope?

You could build many tools here. To keep everyone moving, we'll all build the same three connected stages — the output of each becomes the input of the next, which is how a pipeline creates leverage.

The Three-Stage Pipeline We'll Build

  • Stage 1 — JD → scoring rubric. Paste a job posting, get a weighted rubric — with room for the hiring team's soft context and editable weights.
  • Stage 2 — CV pre-screening. The rubric scores each CV with evidence and surfaces suggestions, not decisions. Recruiters can override, with a reason logged.
  • Stage 3 — Interview kit. Rubric plus assessment produce a tailored kit for one candidate: structure, probing questions on their gaps, and a scorecard.

Why this particular carve-out: these three stages cover the parts of the process that are highest-volume and most repetitive — where automation delivers the most leverage — while keeping humans firmly in charge of the actual hiring decision. The tool prepares the ground; people still do the hiring.

Two Questions to Settle Now

  • Where in each stage will the hiring team inject context the AI doesn't have?
  • What's deliberately out of scope? (Offer letters? Salary recommendations? Final hiring decisions?)

The other design choices — how stages connect, what guardrails to add — you'll answer by building in Section 2 and by facing the stakeholders in Section 3. Don't over-plan them here.

🎯 Scoping discipline: The failure mode is too much ambition. A tool that tries to do everything will do nothing well. Pick the narrow thing that's 80% mechanical and make that excellent. The other 20% is what your recruiters are for.

3Build Stage 1: Role Analysis & Rubric

How do you turn a job description into a scoring rubric?

Build an interface where you paste a JD and get back a structured rubric — weighted categories, specific requirements, red flags.

📎 Note: this stage is a content generation task — closer to drafting than to decision-making. Guardrails can be lighter (a hiring manager will read the rubric before it's used). The heavier controls you discussed in Section 1 kick in at Stage 2.

Your Rubric Should Include

  • 4–6 scoring categories with percentage weights (totalling 100%)
  • Must-have requirements vs nice-to-have for each category
  • Red flags or warning signs to watch for
  • Seniority indicators (what distinguishes senior from junior candidates)

🎛️ Editable weights: AI generates initial weights; hiring managers know things the JD doesn't capture. Build in the ability to manually adjust weights before screening. For example: "The JD emphasises networking, but we actually need someone stronger on cloud — shift 10% from Infrastructure to Cloud Experience." This is where human expertise shapes AI output.

🧠 Design question — how do you capture the hiring manager's soft context? JDs are written for public consumption. They rarely capture the things a hiring manager actually knows about the role: that the team is junior and needs a leader who can mentor; that the previous incumbent struggled with stakeholder management; that the business is about to migrate to Azure so recent Azure experience matters more than the JD implies; that the team sits in a particular cultural context that rewards a certain communication style.

How will your tool let the team add this before the rubric is generated, so it shapes the weights rather than being applied as an override afterwards? Discuss as a group, then build what you decide — the hints below show a couple of ways.

💡 Technical Hint

The simplest working version just displays the rubric as readable text — that's perfectly fine. If you want the stages to pass data cleanly to each other later, you can ask Claude to also produce a structured (JSON) version, but this isn't required for a 2-hour sprint. Pasting the rubric text into Stage 2 works. An "Edit Weights" toggle making percentage fields editable (with validation that they total 100%) is the first point where human judgement can override AI.

For soft context: simplest is a free-text field folded into the prompt ("Additional context from the hiring manager: ..."). One step up: ask a few structured questions (team seniority, recent failure mode, what the JD over- or under-states) and pass the answers in.

🎯 Potential Prompts

Basic structure:

"Build a React component where I paste a job description and get a scoring rubric with weighted categories."

Add a soft-context field:

"Before generating the rubric, ask the hiring manager a few short questions about things the JD might not capture — team seniority, recent failure modes, what the JD might be over- or under-stating. Use the answers to inform the rubric."

Add editability:

"Add an 'Edit Weights' button so I can manually adjust the percentages. Validate they total 100%."

Refine extraction:

"For each category, also show must-haves vs nice-to-haves, and red flags to watch for."

If too generic:

"The rubric is too generic. Make it more specific to the actual requirements in this job description."

4Build Stage 2: CV Scoring

How do you score a CV against your rubric with evidence?

Take a CV, apply the rubric from Stage 1, produce a scored assessment with specific evidence from the CV.

📎 Note: this stage blends prediction and judgement about a person — the kind of task the EU AI Act treats as high-risk in recruitment (Annex III). The stakes are higher here than in Stage 1, and the governance controls you'll design in Section 3 — audit trail, bias testing, human override — apply most forcefully to this stage.

Your Assessment Should Include

  • Overall score (0–100) and tier (Strong Fit / Worth Review / Not a Fit)
  • Score for each rubric category with evidence quotes from the CV
  • Top 3 strengths and top 3 concerns
  • Any red flags matched from the rubric
  • Specific things to verify in interview

Evidence is everything: "Strong technical background" is useless. "7 years managing VMware/Hyper-V environments, led 3 cloud migrations" is actionable. Push for specificity.

🎛️ Manual score override: AI generates scores; reviewers may disagree. Build in ability to adjust individual category scores after the assessment. Log both the original AI score and the adjusted score — an audit trail of human judgement layered on AI analysis.

💡 Technical Hint

Rubric powers the assessment: Pass the Stage 1 rubric into the system prompt for Stage 2 to keep scoring consistent. Add a "hiring manager context" field for things like "the team is junior, so coaching ability matters more than the 5% weight suggests."

Overrides for auditability: log the reason alongside each override — e.g. "AI 65 → reviewer 75: startup experience not captured in keywords." That log is exactly what Legal asks for in Section 3.

🎯 Potential Prompts

Basic screening:

"Add a CV screening stage. Show the rubric on the left, CV input on the right. Score the CV against each category with evidence."

Add override workflow:

"Let me adjust individual category scores manually. Keep both the original AI score and the adjusted score visible, with a required reason field."

5Build Stage 3: Interview Kit

How do you generate questions that probe this specific candidate's gaps?

Generic questions are useless. Questions that probe this candidate's specific gaps and verify their claimed strengths are gold — and that only works because Stages 1 and 2 feed in.

Your Interview Kit Should Include

  • 6–8 tailored questions, each linked to a specific gap or claim to verify
  • For each: what a good answer looks like, plus a follow-up probe
  • A scorecard with rating descriptors (what does 5/5 vs 3/5 vs 1/5 look like?)
💡 Technical Hint

Context is king: The kit generator should receive both the rubric (what we're looking for) and the assessment (what we found). This enables: "Your CV mentions leading cloud migrations, but doesn't specify scale. Walk me through the largest migration you led — how many systems, what timeline, what went wrong?" Vastly more useful than "Tell me about your cloud experience."

🎯 Potential Prompts

Tailored questions:

"Generate interview questions based on this candidate's gaps and concerns. Make them specific to what we found."

Structure and scorecard:

"Add an interview structure with time allocations. For each question, show what a good answer looks like and add a scorecard with rating descriptors."

If too generic:

"These could apply to anyone. Make them specific to this candidate's CV and the gaps we identified."

Section 3 — Evaluation & Reflection

Pressure-test what you built.

7Design Your Evaluation

How would you know if this tool actually works before deploying it?

Building a prototype is step one. Validating it makes good decisions is step two — where most AI projects fail. Design an evaluation that would give you confidence to deploy (or evidence that you shouldn't).

This Is Not a Coding Exercise

You don't need to build the evaluation — you need to design it. What data would you need? What would success look like? What failure modes are you testing for?

Evaluation Dimensions

  • Accuracy: Does it score candidates the way experienced recruiters would?
  • Consistency: Same score to the same CV twice? Similar scores to similar CVs?
  • Calibration: Do "Strong Fit" candidates actually perform better post-hire?
  • Edge cases: Unusual backgrounds, career changers, employment gaps, non-traditional paths?
💡 Evaluation Hint

The gold standard test: Take a recently-filled role where you have (1) the original JD, (2) CVs from candidates you interviewed, (3) your actual hiring decisions. Run the CVs through the tool. Does it rank hires above rejects? Does it flag the same concerns you identified in interviews? Backtesting with real decisions as ground truth.

Edge cases: Find CVs from successful hires with non-traditional backgrounds — career changers, employment gaps, unexpected industries. Does the tool recognise their potential, or penalise them unfairly?

8Stakeholder Concerns & Design Implications

Your tool works — but can you get it approved?

Before deployment, you'll need sign-off from multiple stakeholders. Each has legitimate concerns that affect your system design.

⚖️ Scenario A: Legal Team

"We need to audit every rejection decision. If a candidate challenges our process, we must demonstrate that the decision was fair, consistent, and job-relevant. How does your tool support this?"

Design Questions

  • What data do you need to log for each screening decision?
  • How do you ensure reasoning is traceable and explainable?
  • Can you reconstruct why a candidate was rejected 6 months later?
  • What happens if the AI model is updated — can you still audit old decisions?
💡 Design Levers for Auditability
  • Evidence-based scoring: Every score cites specific CV evidence
  • Versioned rubrics: Store the exact rubric used for each decision
  • Decision logging: Log inputs, outputs, model version
  • Human-in-the-loop: Log which human reviewed/approved/overrode
  • Immutable audit trail: Decisions can't be modified retroactively

🚨 Scenario B: Talent & DEI Team

"We're concerned about gender and racial bias. AI systems have repeatedly been shown to discriminate. We will not approve this tool unless you can demonstrate it doesn't introduce or amplify bias."

Design Questions

  • How would you test for bias without collecting demographic data on candidates?
  • What proxies might inadvertently encode bias? (University names, employment gaps, name patterns?)
  • Should the tool see candidate names at all? University names?
  • How do you distinguish "bias" from "legitimate job-relevant criteria"?
  • What ongoing monitoring would you implement post-deployment?
💡 Design Levers for Bias Mitigation
  • Blind screening: Strip names, photos, universities before AI assessment
  • Synthetic pair testing: Identical CVs with only names/demographics changed — scores should be identical
  • Criteria validation: Every criterion demonstrably job-relevant (legally defensible)
  • Disparate impact analysis: Monitor pass rates by demographic group
  • Regular audits: Third-party bias audits on a regular cadence
  • Human override: AI recommends but humans decide — accountability stays with people

📊 Scenario C: HR Operations

"We process 500 applications per week across 30 open roles. The tool needs to integrate with our ATS, handle multiple job families with different rubrics, and not create more work for our team. What does the operational model look like?"

Design Questions

  • How does the tool integrate with existing systems (ATS, HRIS)?
  • Who maintains rubrics for each job family? How often are they updated?
  • Fully automated? Human review of all? Only edge cases?
  • How do you handle volume spikes (graduate recruitment season)?
  • What training do recruiters need? What's the change management plan?

🎯 Scenario D: Hiring Managers

"I know my team better than any job description captures. Last time HR screened candidates, they rejected someone who would have been perfect because they didn't tick every box. How do I make sure this tool doesn't make the same mistake?"

Design Questions

  • How do hiring managers inject their context and priorities?
  • Can they adjust weights or override criteria for specific searches?
  • How do you balance standardisation (fairness) with customisation (relevance)?
  • What visibility do hiring managers have into why candidates were screened out?
  • Can they "rescue" candidates the tool rejected?

🧭 Four Takeaways Before the Final Debrief

Before the group discussion, pause on what you just did. Four ideas are worth naming — they're the reason this exercise is in the programme.

1. Context engineering is the skill. AI systems don't fail because they aren't smart enough — they fail because they operate without sufficient context. The rubric was only as good as your understanding of what makes a successful hire; the screening was only as useful as the context you passed in. Context engineering is what you were doing every time you pushed back on an output and added specificity.

2. Horizontal vs. vertical. Using AI to brainstorm or draft is a horizontal application — useful but limited. What you built today is a vertical solution: an end-to-end, task-specific pipeline embedded in a real workflow. Harder to build, significantly higher value, because it encodes your domain expertise into the system itself.

3. The product mindset. What makes a "good hire" isn't fixed. Requirements change; the market shifts; your understanding evolves. The context you pass to the model — rubrics, weights, criteria — needs to be easy to update. This is why we built editability in. Treat AI tools as never finished.

4. The jagged frontier. AI performs at expert level on some tasks and makes surprising errors on others — and it's hard to predict which is which. The professionals who thrive with AI can (1) articulate requirements precisely, (2) provide domain context the AI lacks, (3) design appropriate levels of automation, (4) recognise when AI output is wrong, (5) iterate as requirements evolve. These are human skills that become more valuable, not less.

Final Debrief

Let's close out together.

  • Speed: You built a 3-stage application in ~60 minutes that would have taken months with traditional development. Is that good news for your firm, or bad news? What does it change about who gets to build software, and what gets built?
  • Value-add: Where did your domain expertise make the biggest difference? Where did AI handle the heavy lifting?
  • Iteration: How many "try → refine → try again" cycles? What triggered the refinements?
  • Trust: Would you trust this tool to screen real candidates? What's missing?
  • Stakeholders: Which concern was hardest to address? Where did concerns conflict?
  • Governance: What surprised you about the gap between "working prototype" and "deployable system"?
  • Application: What processes in your organisation could benefit from this approach? Who would need to approve them?
